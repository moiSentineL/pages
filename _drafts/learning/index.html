Some lore#

I was a natural achiever in my school days. I didn’t have to work too hard to score well. And it gave me a false sense of confidence, cf. Dunning-Kruger. I thought I was gaming the system quite well, but little did I know that the system was gaming me, and was it in for the long time.

Things started getting overwhelming by the end of 2025, when my grade 12 board exams, entrance tests were near. Mentally, I was brain dead. Academically, my progress almost plateau’d - with a very, very slight upward slope. Intellectually I didn’t know where I was and what I was doing.

And then after botching my first IAT attemp, I realised that there was something significant missing from my life- I didn’t exactly know how to learn, and how to design my life so that it promotes better learning. Sure, I’d been a fanatic for tech, Deep Work, habit systems, and so on for a long time, but things didn’t really connect up until this point.

Turns out that life is just a chemical reaction. You try putting the right chemicals in the right amounts, stirr it a bit, and then you might get just what you want. Of course, the probability of you getting what you want is very close to 0, but never 0 itself. It depends on how you put the chemicals in.

This led me to discovering better learning techniques than just problem-solving, mocks and Anki, get nuanced understanding of how learning occurs, and how one can enable themselves to do so. I put most of my emphasis on the last part because I feel that it’s the most vulnerable part.

People learn for different reasons too. Most people (like me as of this moment) learn to pass an exam, which is pretty much universal at this point. Some people learn because they like the idea of it, and some people learn because their life depends on it.

Regardless of the reason, one naturally find themselves struggling with trying to learn while managing everything in the game called life.

Four Paradigms#

There are four paradigms of learning that affect a life full of learning. They are, in brief:

  • Environmental Design
  • Habit Systems
  • Learning Techniques
  • Resources and Tools.

And as most things are not isolated, these are not either. They are deeply interrelated as you will quickly understand by a quick demonstration.

Environmental Design#

We oscillate between good and bad days, but by optimising our environment and our habit, we can perhaps increase the probability of having “good days” more often.

And by “good days,” I mean days where you are:

  • deeply engaged
  • have momentum
  • energetic
  • ideally, social
  • generating motivation for future “good days”.

Often times, these good days are when you are:

  • doing things on time
  • physically well
  • mentally well
  • emotionally stable
  • functional

Designing your environment gives you a chance to curate and choose what you really need, what really matters to you. We build an external scaffold to rely on, so that we can focus on the things that are truly important.

We want to develop skills like:

  • time and task management
  • curating our physical space (eg. minimalism)
  • establishing time and space based routines
  • tracking time
  • understanding dynamics between ambition and reality.

Habit Systems#

Now that we have an external scaffold, we must also work on our baseline behaviour- our habits. Humans revert back to their muscle memory and habits when things go awry. The goal is to replace those “bad” habits with “better” habits.

Reading James Clear’s Atomic Habits is what I recommend for more information.
